04 Red Flags — Reference Table▸

Any of the following warrant prompt secondary headache evaluation. A single positive feature does not automatically mean imaging — urgency and test choice depend on clinical context.

CategoryFeatures
EmergencyThunderclap onset, new/progressive neurologic deficit, new seizure, altered mental status, intractable nausea/vomiting, fever ± meningismus, papilledema, acute visual loss/diplopia/orbital symptoms, possible CO exposure
Other high-riskNew persistent/daily headache, new or changing pattern, side-locked unilateral headache, postural headache, new visual symptoms, pregnancy/postpartum, age ≥50 at onset, immunosuppression, anticoagulant/relevant medication exposure, morning-predominant headache, oromaxillofacial symptoms
Thunderclap headache is defined as severe headache reaching maximal intensity within one minute — it requires urgent evaluation regardless of how the patient looks in between attacks.
05 Imaging & Testing▸

CT

Best fit: emergency evaluation. Fast, widely available, detects many acute life-threatening causes, and can be combined with CTA/CTV.

MRI

Best fit: nonemergency/elective secondary headache evaluation. More sensitive for many secondary causes, no ionizing radiation.

CTA / MRA

When arterial pathology is suspected — dissection, RCVS, other suspected arterial abnormality.

CTV / MRV

When venous pathology is suspected — cerebral venous thrombosis.

LP — Don't Forget

Consider when:

  • SAH suspected and CT is nondiagnostic
  • Infection suspected
  • Inflammatory/neoplastic process suspected
  • IIH suspected → opening pressure assessment
06 When Testing May Not Be Needed▸

Features supporting a low likelihood of serious secondary disease:

  • Age < 50
  • Previous similar headache attacks
  • No change in established pattern
  • No high-risk comorbidity
  • Normal neurologic examination
  • No concerning new history or examination finding
  • Typical migraine or TTH phenotype
Typical migraine/TTH + normal examination + no red flags → clinical diagnosis may be sufficient. Testing becomes appropriate if high-risk features develop, symptoms change or worsen, or the clinical picture becomes atypical.
07 Phenotype the Headache▸
MigraineUnilateral · Pulsatile · 4–72h

Unilateral, pulsatile, moderate-severe, worse with activity, 4–72 hours.

Associated features:

  • Nausea/vomiting
  • Photophobia
  • Phonophobia
  • Osmophobia
  • ± Aura
Tension-TypeBilateral · Nonthrobbing

Bilateral, mild-moderate, nonthrobbing, relatively featureless.

May have:

  • Neck/jaw discomfort
  • Pericranial tenderness
TACsUnilateral + autonomic features

Strictly unilateral pain with ipsilateral cranial autonomic features. Look for:

  • Lacrimation
  • Conjunctival injection
  • Rhinorrhea/nasal congestion
  • Ptosis
  • Miosis
  • Eyelid edema

09 Clinical History — Resident Checklist▸

Baseline

  • Previous headaches?
  • Age at onset?
  • Frequency?
  • Duration?
  • Number of headache days/month?
  • Number of days/month using acute medications?
  • Previous treatment and response?

Phenotype

  • Onset: sudden or gradual?
  • Location + quality?
  • Associated symptoms?
  • Triggers/relievers?
  • Positional or activity-related?

Don't Forget

  • Vision changes
  • Trauma
  • Pregnancy
  • Immunosuppression
  • Neoplasm
  • Medication changes
  • Sleep/exercise/diet changes
  • Hormonal/menstrual relationship
  • Functional disability

12 Special Situations▸

"It's My Sinuses" — Myth-Buster

When a patient attributes their headache to sinuses, ask: is this actually a recurrent primary headache? Many patients labeled with "sinus headache" fulfill migraine/migrainous headache criteria, and sinus symptoms can accompany migraine.

More suggestive of…Features
Sinus/rhinogenic diseaseProminent rhinogenic symptoms, fever, purulent nasal discharge, nasal obstruction/congestion, prolonged symptoms
MigraineRecurrent self-limited attacks, functional disability, pulsatile pain, photophobia/phonophobia, nausea/vomiting

"Don't Blame the BP"

Don't automatically attribute headache to uncomplicated chronic hypertension. Distinguish severe hypertensive states with neurologic/systemic manifestations from less severe chronic hypertension.

Think hypertensive encephalopathy/PRES when headache occurs with severe BP elevation, altered mental status, visual abnormalities, seizures, or focal neurologic symptoms.

Pregnancy, CO Exposure & Other Named Pathways

Pregnancy headache, CO exposure, infectious headache, ocular/temporal artery, and cerebrovascular pathways are named branch points in Algorithm 1 — each currently routes to a shared "urgent evaluation" endpoint pending dedicated NeuroBureau modules for each.
13 30-Second Resident Recap▸
  1. Thunderclap? → Emergency
  2. Neuro deficit / altered / fever / papilledema / orbital emergency? → Emergency
  3. Other red flags? → Prompt secondary evaluation
  4. No red flags + normal exam? → Phenotype
  5. Migraine / TTH? → Often clinical diagnosis
  6. TAC / new daily persistent / atypical primary phenotype? → Consider imaging at initial diagnosis
  7. Need a test? Emergency → CT ± CTA/CTV. Nonemergency → MRI ± MRA/MRV. Specific indications → LP / targeted labs.
